Rwanda · 1700

The Kingdom of Rwanda

By the early modern period Rwanda has developed a centralized monarchy ruled by the mwami and an aristocratic court. Hutu, Tutsi and Twa identities exist before colonial rule but are shaped by region, status, occupation and politics rather than the rigid racial categories later imposed by Europeans.

Barbados · 1660

Sugar plantations in the Caribbean

Barbadian sugar estates combine wind- or animal-powered mills with boiling houses where cane juice is processed through heated copper vessels. Enslaved workers face long hours, heat, machinery and other serious hazards throughout cane cultivation and sugar processing.

Bolivia · 1650

Potosí

Potosí becomes one of the Spanish Empire's great silver-mining centers. From 1573 the colonial mita requires more than 200 Indigenous communities to send roughly one-seventh of their adult men for rotating labor service. Mining, mercury exposure, coercion and disease impose severe human costs, but later claims of eight million deaths are not a reliable demographic estimate.

Mali · 1600

The Azalai salt caravan crosses the Sahara

The Azalai salt caravan sets out from Timbuktu across the Sahara to the mines of Taoudenni and back. Long files of camels carry grain and goods north and return laden with slabs of salt.

Angola · 1700

The Ovimbundu of the Bié plateau

The Ovimbundu build a cluster of kingdoms on the Bié plateau of the central highlands, farming the uplands and trading toward the coast. They become one of the most numerous peoples of the region.

And far away…

Antigua and Barbuda · 1700

Barbuda, the provisioning ground

Barbuda feeds the Codrington sugar machine: its enslaved community raises cattle, grows food and salvages wrecks for the Antigua estates. A durable legend calls it a 'slave-breeding' farm; historians find no breeding program, but its people are indeed kept as a reserve of labor to be shipped wherever cane needs hands.

Argentina · 1700

The Mapuche of the southern plains

From the seventeenth century onward, Mapuche-speaking groups increasingly move and trade east of the Andes into the northern Patagonian and Pampas regions. Horse culture, exchange and warfare reshape identities and political networks in a process later called the Araucanization of the Pampas.
